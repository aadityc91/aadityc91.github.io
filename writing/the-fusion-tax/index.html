The Fusion Tax: When Hybrid Search Hurts

Hybrid retrieval wins on average, and the average hides the queries it makes worse. What Arun Agarwal and I measured across seven corpora and five encoders, and how to decide whether to fuse.

Aaditya Chauhan · October 2026 · about 15 minutes, plus time to play with the figures

TL;DR
  • Fusing BM25 with dense retrieval beat dense search alone on average on all seven corpora we tested. It still ranked relevant documents below dense-only on 8–21% of queries, and below the better of its own two inputs on 13–48%.
  • RRF, the fusion most search stacks recommend, is the weaker choice when one retriever is clearly stronger. There, a dense-weighted score blend wins. RRF earns its place when the two retrievers are evenly matched.
  • A bigger or newer encoder doesn't make the tax go away, and cheap per-query routing doesn't transfer across corpora. What sets the tax is the balance between your two retrievers.
  • Measure that balance on a few hundred labeled queries from your own traffic, pick the fusion method from it, and keep dense-only on the table.

Ask how to build retrieval for a RAG system and you'll be told to go hybrid: run BM25 and a vector search, then fuse the two lists. At the time of writing that's the recommended setup in Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search and LangChain, and reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) is the fusion they recommend or ship as the only option. The advice rests on averages. Over a set of queries, the fused list usually beats either input.

Averages are where per-query losses hide. A system can get better on average while getting worse on one query in five, and the person who asked that query never sees your average. So Arun Agarwal and I asked a narrower question in The Fusion Tax, our paper in IEEE Access: on a given query, how often does fusion rank relevant documents below the better of its own two inputs, and below the single retriever you'd otherwise deploy? We treated “don't fuse” as a real option, not a baseline to beat.

This post is the practitioner's version: what we found, what I'd do differently because of it, and six figures to play with. The figures run on the paper's actual per-query results, not a simulation.

One query, three rankings

Start with one real query from SciFact, a benchmark of scientific claims: “Silencing of Bcl2 is important for the maintenance and progression of tumors.” Dense retrieval ranks the one relevant abstract first. BM25 ranks it 29th. Watch what each fusion method does with that disagreement, then try the other two queries.

RRF only sees ranks. It gives each document 1/(60 + rank) from each list and adds them up. A document that dense ranks first and BM25 ranks 29th gets 1/61 + 1/89, and a document both lists put somewhere in their top ten can beat that. RRF can't tell that dense was confident and BM25 was guessing.

Weighted fusion sees scores. It rescales each list's scores to 0–1 and blends them, here with dense weighted 0.7. Dense's confident first place survives.

But a weight is a bet on one side. In the second query, BM25 ranks the relevant abstract first and dense ranks it 22nd. Now the 0.7 weight pushes the document BM25 had right down to fifth. The third query is the case fusion is built for: both lists have the relevant abstract partway down, and their agreement lifts it above where either had it.

What we measured

Seven BEIR corpora: SciFact (scientific claims), FiQA (financial questions), NFCorpus (medical), ArguAna (counter-arguments), SciDocs (citation prediction), TREC-COVID (50 COVID questions) and Quora (duplicate questions). The sparse side is the standard Anserini BM25, which reproduces the published BEIR numbers to four decimals. The dense side is five encoders from 22M to 596M parameters across three model generations: MiniLM, BGE-small, BGE-base, BGE-large, and Qwen3-Embedding from 2025. The detailed per-query analysis uses BGE-small. We tried three fusion methods: RRF (k = 60), min–max normalized score interpolation with a dense weight of 0.7 unless stated, and CombMNZ. The metric is nDCG@10.

There are two ways to count a loss. The oracle tax is the share of queries on which fusion scores below the better of BM25 and dense for that query. It's an upper bound, because collecting it would mean knowing in advance which retriever wins each query. The deployable tax is the share on which fusion scores below dense-only, the single retriever you'd ship instead (dense was the stronger one on every corpus). That's the harm a team that simply chose dense search would have avoided. We always report it next to the share of queries fusion helped, because harm without benefit is half a ledger.

The tax, query by query

Figure 2 puts every query on one chart: BM25's nDCG@10 across, dense's up. Queries in the strip along the left edge are ones BM25 missed entirely, and queries along the bottom are ones dense missed. Each dot is colored by what fusion did to it.

Harm concentrates where the retrievers disagree. On queries only one retriever finds, fusion mixes in the other retriever's noise. With the 0.7 dense weight, fusion hurts 87–95% of the queries only BM25 found, and 38–65% of the queries only dense found. These queries are a minority, at most 29% of any corpus.

Most of the tax is in plain sight anyway. Queries both retrievers find are the large majority, and fusion reorders them in both directions. That group holds 38–72% of all the hurt queries, so fixing only the disagreements would leave most of the tax in place.

Dense rarely misses what BM25 finds. Queries only BM25 covers never exceed 7% of a corpus. That asymmetry is part of why a dense-heavy weight wins on average: it gives up the small BM25-only group to protect everything else.

Fusion wins on average and still costs you

Figure 3 is the ledger for every corpus: how many queries fusion hurt and helped, by how much, and the net.

Against dense-only, weighted fusion is a good trade on average. It helps 12–72% of queries and hurts 8–21%. On every corpus the average gain on a helped query matches or beats the average loss on a hurt one, so the net is positive everywhere, by 0.008 to 0.051 nDCG@10.

But the losses aren't rounding errors. A hurt query loses 0.04–0.21 nDCG@10 on average, the same order as the gains. Against the better of the two retrievers, 13–48% of queries pay. Counting only material losses, above 0.05, it's still 10–34%.

RRF's ledger is worse. It hurts 15–33% of queries against dense-only, and its net is negative on five of the seven corpora. It comes out ahead only on NFCorpus and TREC-COVID, the two corpora where BM25 and dense are about equally good.

Under loss-averse scoring, the downside is significant. Risk-sensitive evaluation (TRisk) counts a loss more heavily than a gain of the same size. With losses weighted six times as heavily, interpolation is significantly riskier than dense-only on six of the seven corpora, even though it wins on the mean.

It's mostly a ranking problem, not a recall problem. Fusion's recall@10 matches or beats dense-only's on every corpus, and recall drops on only 1–7% of queries. For most hurt queries the relevant documents are still in the top ten, just lower. That matters if whatever consumes the list cares about order: a reranker with a small budget, a UI that shows three results, a reader that leans on the first passage. It isn't all reordering, though. Of the queries fusion hurts, 10–43% also lose a relevant document from the top ten, and 77 queries across the seven corpora lose every one.

My recommendation: report fusion as a ledger, not an average: the share of queries helped, the share hurt and the mean size of each, against the single retriever you'd otherwise ship. If you add only one line to your evaluation dashboard, make it the share of queries where hybrid ranks below dense-only.

RRF is not a safe default

RRF is popular for a good reason. It needs no score calibration, and BM25 scores and cosine similarities live on scales that don't compare. But on the five corpora where dense was clearly stronger, leading by about 0.04–0.18 nDCG@10, dense-weighted interpolation beat RRF in aggregate, significantly on four. RRF also trailed plain dense search on all five.

Two things hold RRF back there. It weights both lists equally, and it throws away the scores, so a confident first place counts the same as a lucky one. A weighted RRF with the same 0.7/0.3 split closes 25–73% of the gap to interpolation, so each explains roughly half.

Where the two retrievers are balanced, RRF wins. On TREC-COVID (BM25 0.656, dense 0.666), RRF and CombMNZ scored 0.801 and 0.805 against 0.717 for dense-heavy interpolation, and helped 70–74% of queries. On NFCorpus (0.325 against 0.337) the three methods tie within 0.003. The metasearch literature predicted this split long ago: blend scores when one side is reliably stronger and its confidence is worth keeping, and fuse ranks when neither side should dominate. The engines disagree too. Elasticsearch recommends RRF, OpenSearch defaults to min–max score blending, and Weaviate switched its default from rank fusion to score fusion in version 1.24.

My recommendation: don't take your engine's fusion default on faith. If dense leads by a clear margin on your data, use a dense-weighted score blend. If the two are neck and neck, RRF is a good choice.

Turning the weight

If you blend scores, you have to pick the weight. Figure 4 sweeps it from all BM25 to all dense.

The best weight is in the middle, and it travels. The in-sample optimum was 0.7 on four corpora, 0.6 on NFCorpus, 0.8 on ArguAna and 0.5 on TREC-COVID, and never 1.0: some blending always beat dense alone on the mean. Choosing the weight on six corpora and applying it to the seventh picked 0.6 or 0.7 every time, at a cost of at most 0.014 nDCG@10 against the in-sample best on every corpus but TREC-COVID (0.031).

The best average isn't the safest weight. TREC-COVID shows the trade. Moving from 0.7 to 0.5 raises its mean nDCG@10 from 0.717 to 0.748, and raises the share of queries that land below dense-only from 14% to 26%. Which you want depends on whether your users feel the average or the worst case.

My recommendation: if dense leads, start at a dense weight of 0.6–0.7, set the final value by cross-validation on your labeled queries, and measure the tax on queries you didn't tune on.

A bigger encoder won't fix it

The obvious objection is that the tax is a symptom of weak embeddings. So we scaled the dense side: BGE-small, BGE-base and BGE-large (33M to 335M parameters, one family), plus MiniLM (22M, from 2021) and Qwen3-Embedding (596M, from 2025). Step through them in Figure 5.

Better encoders raise the scores, not the floor. Across the BGE family's tenfold increase in parameters, the interpolation tax on SciFact, FiQA, NFCorpus and ArguAna moves by at most four points, while nDCG@10 climbs. At BGE-large, interpolation still hurts 17–40% of queries on six corpora. Qwen3-Embedding, the newest and largest model, pays 14–43%, a range that overlaps every older and smaller model's. Where its tax differs, it follows the balance between the two retrievers rather than the encoder's quality: it's highest on NFCorpus, where Qwen3 barely leads BM25, and lowest on TREC-COVID and Quora, where it leads by a lot. Fusion still beat dense-only on average on 33 of the 35 encoder–corpus pairs.

A better BM25 doesn't fix it either. Swapping BM25 for SPLADE, a stronger learned-sparse retriever, left interpolation's tax at 14–41% on six corpora and 60% on TREC-COVID. That tax comes with score blending itself. RRF's tax does respond to the quality of the lexical side, which fits a method that can't tell a weak list's confident-looking ranks from a strong one's, but with SPLADE RRF still hurt 19–46% of queries.

My recommendation: when you upgrade the embedding model, re-measure the balance. A new encoder changes which fusion method and weight you should use, not whether the tax exists.

Can you route around it?

If fusion fails on predictable queries, you could fuse only when it helps. A perfect per-query router would gain 0.02–0.05 nDCG@10 over static fusion on every corpus, so the prize is real. We tried to collect it with 17 cheap features per query (query length, term rarity, score margins, overlap between the two lists, and classic query-performance predictors such as NQC and WIG) feeding a gradient-boosted classifier.

Within a corpus, failure is weakly predictable. The mean held-out AUC was 0.63, up to 0.80 on Quora. Across corpora, it isn't reliably. Trained on six corpora and tested on the seventh, AUC averaged 0.59 and ranged from chance (0.49 on NFCorpus) to 0.74. A router that switched queries to dense-only never beat static interpolation on a held-out corpus. On three corpora it routed nothing, and on the two where it routed a large share of queries it lost a little.

The signals change meaning from corpus to corpus. The dense retriever's top-1 margin is the clearest case. Where dense dominates, a confident dense top hit means fusion can only dilute it, so the query is safe. On TREC-COVID, where BM25 is as strong as dense, the same confident hit predicts a failure, because the dense-heavy weight throws away BM25's evidence. Eleven of the 17 features point toward failure on some corpus and away from it on another. A router trained on one balance learns the wrong lesson for the next.

My recommendation: pick one fusion policy per corpus. If you want per-query routing, validate it on a corpus it wasn't trained on before you trust it.

Does a reranker pay the tax?

Most production stacks put a cross-encoder after retrieval, so maybe first-stage order doesn't matter. We reranked the top 30 candidates from each method with BGE-reranker-v2-m3.

It helps only where the reranker is good at the corpus. On the three corpora where it improved every method (SciFact, FiQA and TREC-COVID), the share of queries where fusion finished below dense-only fell by a third on SciFact and not at all on the other two. Even there it mostly reshuffled who pays: on FiQA it repaired 75 of 95 hurt queries and newly hurt 81 others. On ArguAna and SciDocs the reranker made every method worse, and the tax “disappeared” only because every list converged on the reranker's own ordering.

After a good reranker, the second retriever adds little. Once reranked, interpolated candidates scored within 0.011 nDCG@10 of dense-only candidates on six corpora, with the balanced TREC-COVID the exception. The costs are lopsided. On one consumer machine (an Apple M5), a BM25 query took 1.1–4.3 ms, while the cross-encoder took about 1.6 s per query for 30 candidates. BM25 is the cheap lever and the reranker the expensive one. These are results for one reranker at one depth, so treat them as a measurement, not a ceiling.

My recommendation: if you already run a reliable reranker, spend your effort validating it on each corpus rather than tuning fusion. If you can't afford a reranker, fusion with a weight set from your corpus's balance is the cheap win.

Deciding for your corpus

Here is the paper's guidance as a calculator. Enter what you measured on your own labeled sample, or start from one of the seven corpora.

  1. Measure the balance first. Sample 200–400 real queries, stratified across your traffic, and judge at least the pooled top ten of both retrievers. Compare BM25 and dense nDCG@10 per query. In our data the per-query differences had standard deviations of 0.17–0.36, which puts the 95% interval on the gap at about ±0.02–0.05 with 200 queries.
  2. Pick the method from the balance. If dense leads clearly (by 0.04 or more in our data, against about 0.01 on the balanced corpora), use dense-weighted interpolation at 0.6–0.7, set by cross-validation. If they're balanced, use RRF, CombMNZ or interpolation near 0.5.
  3. Keep dense-only on the table. Fusion's edge over dense-only was 0.008–0.051 nDCG@10, while 8–21% of queries lost 0.04–0.21. If your consumer is rank-sensitive and you care about the worst case, dense-only may be the better product.
  4. Don't expect a new encoder to settle it. Re-measure the balance whenever you change the encoder or the corpus shifts.
  5. Budget the second retriever against the reranker. BM25 costs milliseconds per query; a cross-encoder costs a second or more.

Closing thoughts

Hybrid retrieval isn't wrong. On average it won on every corpus we tested. But an average is a sum of wins and losses, and the losses land on specific queries and the people who asked them. Count both sides, choose the fusion method from your corpus's balance, and give dense-only a fair hearing.

What this doesn't cover. We measured first-stage retrieval and one reranking stage, not generated answers, so how much of the tax reaches a RAG answer depends on how rank-sensitive your reader is. The reranking results are for one cross-encoder at depth 30. TREC-COVID has only 50 queries, so its numbers are noisy, and the multi-million-document BEIR corpora were left out to keep everything runnable on a CPU.
