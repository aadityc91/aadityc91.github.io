The Fusion Tax: When Hybrid Search Hurts

Hybrid search wins on average and loses on more individual queries than you'd think. What Arun Agarwal and I found across seven corpora and five encoders, and how I'd decide whether to fuse.

Aaditya Chauhan · October 2026 · about 9 minutes, plus time to play with the figures

TL;DR
  • Fusing BM25 with dense retrieval beat dense alone on average on every corpus we tested, yet it ranked relevant documents below dense-only on 8–21% of queries and below the better of its two inputs on 13–48%.
  • RRF is the weaker choice when one retriever is clearly stronger; a dense-weighted score blend wins there. RRF earns its keep when the two are evenly matched.
  • Bigger encoders and cheap per-query routing don't fix it. Measure your retrievers' balance on a few hundred labeled queries, pick the fusion method from it, and keep dense-only on the table.

If you've built retrieval for RAG lately, someone has told you to go hybrid: run BM25 and a vector search, then fuse the lists, usually with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF). I've given that advice myself, in an InfoQ article in June. But it rests on averages, and averages hide things. A system can improve on average while getting worse on one query in five.

So in The Fusion Tax, our IEEE Access paper, Arun Agarwal and I asked a per-query question: how often does fusion rank the relevant documents below the better of its own two inputs, or below the one retriever you'd ship if you didn't fuse at all? That per-query loss is what we call the fusion tax: a ranking loss on a single query, not a compute bill. We treated “don't fuse” as a real option. The figures below run on the paper's real per-query results.

Start with one query

Here's a real SciFact claim: “Silencing of Bcl2 is important for the maintenance and progression of tumors.” Dense retrieval ranks the one relevant abstract first; BM25 ranks it 29th. Watch what each fusion method does, then try the other two queries.

RRF only sees ranks. The abstract gets 1/61 + 1/89, and any document both lists put in their top ten beats that, because RRF can't tell that dense was confident and BM25 was guessing. Weighted fusion blends normalized scores (dense 0.7 here), so dense's confident first place survives.

But a weight is a bet on one side. In the second query BM25 has the answer first and dense has it 22nd, and the 0.7 weight pushes it down to fifth. The third query is what fusion is built for: both lists have the answer partway down, and agreeing on it lifts it to the top.

What we measured

Seven BEIR corpora (SciFact, FiQA, NFCorpus, ArguAna, SciDocs, TREC-COVID and Quora), the standard Anserini BM25, and five dense encoders from 22M to 596M parameters. The per-query analysis uses BGE-small. We fused with RRF (k = 60), min–max score interpolation (dense weight 0.7 unless I say otherwise) and CombMNZ, scored with nDCG@10.

The BM25 detail sounds like a footnote, but it isn't. Ours reproduces the published BEIR numbers to four decimals. A weak BM25 inflates the tax, at least for RRF, which would have made our point for us. We didn't want to win that way.

We counted losses two ways. The oracle tax is the share of queries where fusion scores below the better of the two retrievers on that query, an upper bound you could only collect by knowing the winner in advance. The deployable tax is the share where it scores below dense-only, the retriever you'd ship instead (dense was the stronger of the two on every corpus). We always report it next to the share fusion helped.

The tax, query by query

Figure 2 plots every query: BM25's nDCG@10 across, dense's up. The left and bottom strips hold queries one retriever missed completely.

The hurt rate is highest where the retrievers disagree. With the 0.7 dense weight, fusion hurts 87–95% of the queries only BM25 found and 38–65% of those only dense found. But those are at most 29% of any corpus. Queries both retrievers find hold 38–72% of all the hurt queries, so fixing the disagreements alone leaves most of the tax in place.

Dense also rarely misses what BM25 finds: queries only BM25 covers never go above 7% of a corpus. That's part of why a dense-heavy weight wins on average. It gives up the small BM25-only group to protect everything else.

Winning on average, losing on queries

Figure 3 is the ledger for every corpus.

Against dense-only, weighted fusion is a good trade on average: it helps 12–72% of queries, hurts 8–21%, and comes out ahead on every corpus by 0.008 to 0.051 nDCG@10. RRF looks worse. It hurts 15–33% of queries against dense-only and loses on average on five corpora.

The losses aren't rounding errors, though. A hurt query loses 0.04–0.21 nDCG@10 on average, the same order as the gains, and against the better of the two retrievers 13–48% of queries pay. Count only losses above 0.05 and it's still 10–34%. Weight losses six times as heavily, as risk-sensitive evaluation (TRisk) can, and interpolation is significantly riskier than dense-only on six of the seven corpora.

It's mostly about order. Recall@10 drops on only 1–7% of queries, so for most hurt queries the relevant documents are still in the top ten, just lower. That matters when a small-budget reranker, a three-result UI or an LLM reads the list. And 10–43% of hurt queries do lose a relevant document from the top ten; 77 queries across the seven corpora lose every one.

Track fusion as a ledger. If your dashboard has room for one more line, make it the share of queries where hybrid ranks below dense-only.

Is RRF really the safe default?

This is where I walk back some of my own advice. In the InfoQ piece I recommended RRF over weighted blending because it needs no score calibration. On the five corpora where dense clearly led (by about 0.04–0.18 nDCG@10), dense-weighted interpolation beat RRF at the usual k = 60 default, significantly on four, and RRF trailed plain dense search on all five. RRF weights both lists equally and throws the scores away, so a confident first place counts the same as a lucky one. Giving RRF the same 0.7/0.3 weights closes 25–73% of the gap to interpolation, so each of those explains roughly half.

Where the retrievers are balanced, RRF wins. On TREC-COVID (BM25 0.656, dense 0.666), RRF scored 0.801 against 0.717 for dense-heavy interpolation and helped 70–74% of queries; on NFCorpus the three methods tie within 0.003. Even the engines disagree: Elasticsearch recommends RRF, while OpenSearch defaults to min–max score blending and Weaviate switched its default to score fusion in version 1.24.

Don't take your engine's default on faith. If dense clearly leads, blend scores with a dense weight. If they're neck and neck, RRF is fine.

Picking the weight

Figure 4 sweeps the dense weight from all BM25 to all dense.

The best weight sits in the middle and travels well. The in-sample optimum ranged from 0.5 to 0.8 and was never 1.0, and a weight chosen on six corpora (0.6 or 0.7 every time) cost at most 0.014 nDCG@10 on the seventh, except TREC-COVID (0.031). Pick the weight on held-out queries, as I recommend below, and expect a bit more harm than at a fixed 0.7: up to 25–26% of queries below dense-only instead of 21%. But the best average isn't the safest weight. On TREC-COVID, moving from 0.7 to 0.5 raises mean nDCG@10 from 0.717 to 0.748, and the share of queries below dense-only from 14% to 26%.

My starting point: 0.6–0.7 if dense leads, set by cross-validation, with the tax measured on queries you didn't tune on.

Won't a better encoder fix this?

It's the obvious objection, so we scaled the dense side from MiniLM (22M) through the BGE family to Qwen3-Embedding (596M, from 2025).

Better encoders raise the scores. They don't lower the tax. At BGE-large, interpolation still hurts 17–40% of queries on six corpora, and Qwen3-Embedding pays 14–43%. Where Qwen3's tax moves, it follows the balance between the retrievers: highest on NFCorpus, where it barely leads BM25, and lowest on TREC-COVID and Quora, where it leads by a lot. Fusion still beat dense-only on average on 33 of the 35 encoder–corpus pairs. Swapping BM25 for SPLADE doesn't fix it either: interpolation's tax stays at 14–41% on six corpora. So when you upgrade the encoder, re-measure the balance. It changes which fusion method you should use, not whether there's a tax.

Can you route around it?

A perfect per-query router would gain 0.02–0.05 nDCG@10 over static fusion, so we tried to build one from 17 cheap query features. Within a corpus, failure is weakly predictable (mean AUC 0.63). Across corpora, AUC ranged from chance (0.49) to 0.74, and a router that switched queries to dense-only never beat static interpolation on a held-out corpus. On three corpora it didn't route a single query, and on the two where it routed a lot of them it lost a little.

The signals flip meaning. A confident dense top hit marks a safe query where dense dominates, and predicts a failure on TREC-COVID, where BM25 is as strong. Eleven of the 17 features point in opposite directions on different corpora. I'd pick one fusion policy per corpus and leave it there.

Does a reranker pay the tax?

Most production stacks put a cross-encoder after retrieval, so does first-stage order even matter? We reranked the top 30 candidates of each method with BGE-reranker-v2-m3, and it paid the tax only partly, and only where it was good at the corpus. The share of queries where fusion finished below dense-only fell by a third on SciFact and not at all on FiQA or TREC-COVID, and on FiQA it repaired 75 of 95 hurt queries while newly hurting 81. On ArguAna and SciDocs it made every method worse.

After a good reranker, the second retriever adds little: interpolated candidates scored within 0.011 nDCG@10 of dense-only ones on six corpora. Meanwhile, on our test machine, BM25 cost 1.1–4.3 ms a query and the cross-encoder about 1.6 s. This is one reranker at one depth, so read it as a measurement, not a ceiling. If you already run a reranker you trust, validate it per corpus rather than tuning fusion.

What I'd do on your corpus

Here's the paper's guidance as a calculator. Plug in your own numbers or start from one of the seven corpora.

  1. Measure the balance. Label 200–400 real queries from your traffic, judging the pooled top ten of both retrievers, and compare BM25 and dense nDCG@10 query by query. Our per-query differences had standard deviations of 0.17–0.36, which gives a 95% interval of about ±0.02–0.05 on the gap at 200 queries. If that interval straddles the 0.04 line, label more. And when you evaluate fusion itself, judge the documents it pulls in from outside both top tens too.
  2. Pick the method from it. Dense ahead by 0.04 or more: dense-weighted interpolation at 0.6–0.7. About even: RRF, CombMNZ or interpolation near 0.5.
  3. Keep dense-only on the table, especially if your consumer is rank-sensitive and you care about the worst case.
  4. Re-measure whenever the encoder or the corpus changes.

Closing thoughts

Hybrid retrieval isn't wrong, and I'd still reach for it first in most systems. But an average is a sum of wins and losses, and the losses land on specific queries and the people who typed them. Count both sides, pick the fusion method from your corpus's balance, and give dense-only a fair hearing.

What this doesn't cover. We measured retrieval and one reranking stage, not generated answers, so how much of the tax reaches a RAG answer depends on how rank-sensitive your reader is. The reranking results are for one cross-encoder at depth 30, and TREC-COVID has only 50 queries.
