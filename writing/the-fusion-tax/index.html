The Fusion Tax: When Hybrid Search Hurts

Hybrid search wins on average. On individual queries it loses more often than you'd think. What Arun Agarwal and I found across seven corpora and five encoders, and how I'd decide whether to fuse.

Aaditya Chauhan · October 2026 · about 15 minutes, plus time to play with the figures

TL;DR
  • On every corpus we tested, fusing BM25 with dense retrieval beat dense search alone on average. It still ranked relevant documents below dense-only on 8–21% of queries, and below the better of its own two inputs on 13–48%.
  • RRF is the weaker choice when one retriever is clearly stronger. A dense-weighted score blend wins there. RRF earns its keep when the two retrievers are evenly matched.
  • A bigger or newer encoder doesn't make the tax go away, and cheap per-query routing doesn't carry over from one corpus to the next. What drives the tax is the balance between your two retrievers.
  • So measure that balance on a few hundred labeled queries from your own traffic, pick the fusion method from it, and keep dense-only on the table.

If you've built retrieval for a RAG system in the last couple of years, someone has told you to go hybrid: run BM25 and a vector search, then fuse the two lists. Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search and LangChain all point you there, and the fusion they recommend (or the only one they ship) is reciprocal rank fusion, RRF.

I've given that advice myself. In June I wrote an InfoQ article arguing for hybrid retrieval with RRF, mostly because it sidesteps the score normalization problem. I still think hybrid is the right call more often than not. But that advice, mine included, rests on averages: over a set of queries, the fused list usually beats either input.

Averages hide things. A system can get better on average while getting worse on one query in five, and the person who typed that query never sees your average. So Arun Agarwal and I asked a narrower question in The Fusion Tax, our paper in IEEE Access. On a single query, how often does fusion rank the relevant documents below the better of its own two inputs? And how often below the one retriever you'd ship if you didn't fuse at all? We treated “don't fuse” as a real option.

This post is the practitioner's version: what we found, what I'd do differently because of it, and six figures you can play with. They run on the paper's real per-query results.

Start with one query

Here's a real query from SciFact, a benchmark of scientific claims: “Silencing of Bcl2 is important for the maintenance and progression of tumors.” Dense retrieval puts the one relevant abstract first. BM25 puts it 29th. Watch what each fusion method does with that disagreement, then try the other two queries.

RRF only looks at ranks. Each list gives a document 1/(60 + rank), and the two are added up. So the abstract that dense ranked first and BM25 ranked 29th gets 1/61 + 1/89, and a document both lists put somewhere in their top ten beats it. RRF has no way of knowing that dense was confident and BM25 was guessing.

Weighted fusion looks at scores. It rescales each list to 0–1 and blends them, here with dense weighted 0.7, and dense's confident first place survives.

But a weight is a bet on one side. Switch to the second query: BM25 has the relevant abstract first, dense has it 22nd, and now the 0.7 weight pushes BM25's right answer down to fifth. The third query is the case fusion was built for. Both lists have the relevant abstract somewhere in the middle, and agreeing on it lifts it above where either one had it.

What we measured

Seven BEIR corpora: SciFact (scientific claims), FiQA (financial questions), NFCorpus (medical), ArguAna (counter-arguments), SciDocs (citation prediction), TREC-COVID (50 COVID questions) and Quora (duplicate questions).

For the sparse side we used the standard Anserini BM25, which reproduces the published BEIR numbers to four decimals. That sounds like a footnote, but it isn't. A weak BM25 inflates the tax, at least for RRF, which would have made our point for us. We didn't want to win that way.

The dense side is five encoders from 22M to 596M parameters, across three model generations: MiniLM, BGE-small, BGE-base, BGE-large and the 2025 Qwen3-Embedding. The detailed per-query analysis uses BGE-small. We fused with three methods: RRF (k = 60), min–max normalized score interpolation (dense weight 0.7 unless I say otherwise) and CombMNZ. Everything is scored with nDCG@10.

There are two ways to count a loss, and the difference matters. The oracle tax is the share of queries where fusion scores below the better of BM25 and dense for that query. Treat it as an upper bound, because collecting it means knowing ahead of time which retriever wins each query. The deployable tax is the share where fusion scores below dense-only, the single retriever you'd actually ship instead (dense was the stronger of the two on every corpus). That's the harm a team that simply picked dense search would have avoided. We always report it next to the share of queries fusion helped. Harm without the benefit is only half the story.

The tax, query by query

Figure 2 puts every query on one chart, BM25's nDCG@10 across and dense's up. The strip on the left edge holds queries BM25 missed completely, and the strip along the bottom holds the ones dense missed. Each dot is colored by what fusion did to it.

The hurt rate is highest where the retrievers disagree. When only one of them finds something, fusion mixes in the other one's noise. With the 0.7 dense weight, fusion hurts 87–95% of the queries only BM25 found and 38–65% of the queries only dense found. Those queries are a minority, though: at most 29% of any corpus.

The part that's easy to miss is how much of the tax sits in plain sight. Queries both retrievers find are the large majority, and fusion reshuffles them in both directions. That group holds 38–72% of all the hurt queries. Fix only the disagreements and you leave most of the tax where it is.

Dense also rarely misses what BM25 finds. Queries only BM25 covers never go above 7% of a corpus, and that asymmetry is part of why a dense-heavy weight wins on average. It gives up the small BM25-only group to protect everything else.

Winning on average, losing on queries

Figure 3 is the ledger: for every corpus, how many queries fusion hurt and helped, by how much, and the net.

Against dense-only, weighted fusion is a good trade on average. It helps 12–72% of queries and hurts 8–21%. On every corpus the average gain on a helped query matches or beats the average loss on a hurt one, so the net comes out positive everywhere, by 0.008 to 0.051 nDCG@10.

The losses aren't rounding errors, though. A hurt query loses 0.04–0.21 nDCG@10 on average, the same order as the gains. Against the better of the two retrievers, 13–48% of queries pay. Count only the losses above 0.05 and it's still 10–34%.

RRF's ledger looks worse. It hurts 15–33% of queries against dense-only, and its net is negative on five of the seven corpora. It only comes out ahead on NFCorpus and TREC-COVID, the two corpora where BM25 and dense are about equally good.

Score it the way a loss-averse team would and the downside becomes significant. Risk-sensitive evaluation (TRisk) counts a loss more heavily than a gain of the same size. With losses weighted six times as heavily, interpolation comes out significantly riskier than dense-only on six of the seven corpora, even though it wins on the mean.

Most of this is about order rather than recall. Fusion's recall@10 matches or beats dense-only's on every corpus, and recall drops on only 1–7% of queries. For most hurt queries the relevant documents are still in the top ten, just lower down. That matters when whatever reads the list cares about order: a reranker with a small budget, a UI that shows three results, an LLM that leans on the first passage. It isn't all reordering, though. Of the queries fusion hurts, 10–43% also lose a relevant document from the top ten, and 77 queries across the seven corpora lose every single one.

If you take one thing from this section, track fusion as a ledger: the share of queries helped, the share hurt and how big each move is, against the single retriever you'd otherwise ship. If your dashboard has room for one more line, make it the share of queries where hybrid ranks below dense-only.

Is RRF really the safe default?

This is where I have to walk back some of my own advice. In the InfoQ piece I recommended RRF over weighted blending. RRF needs no score calibration, and BM25 scores and cosine similarities live on scales that don't compare, so it felt like the safe pick. I also argued that tuning weights per query isn't practical at production scale, and that part still holds (more on routing below). A single fixed weight per corpus turned out to be a different story.

On the five corpora where dense was clearly stronger, leading by about 0.04–0.18 nDCG@10, dense-weighted interpolation beat RRF in aggregate, significantly on four of them. RRF also trailed plain dense search on all five.

Two things hold RRF back there. It weights both lists equally, and it throws the scores away, so a confident first place counts the same as a lucky one. When we gave RRF the same 0.7/0.3 split, it closed 25–73% of the gap to interpolation, so each of those explains roughly half.

Where the two retrievers are balanced, RRF wins. On TREC-COVID (BM25 0.656, dense 0.666), RRF and CombMNZ scored 0.801 and 0.805 against 0.717 for dense-heavy interpolation, and helped 70–74% of queries. On NFCorpus (0.325 against 0.337) all three methods land within 0.003 of each other.

None of this is new, by the way. The old metasearch literature predicted the split: blend scores when one side is reliably stronger and its confidence is worth keeping, and fuse ranks when neither side should dominate. Even the engines disagree. Elasticsearch recommends RRF, OpenSearch defaults to min–max score blending, and Weaviate switched its default from rank fusion to score fusion in version 1.24.

So don't take your engine's default on faith. If dense leads by a clear margin on your data, use a dense-weighted score blend. If the two are neck and neck, RRF is a perfectly good choice.

Picking the weight

If you blend scores, you have to pick the weight. Figure 4 sweeps it from all BM25 to all dense.

The best weight sits somewhere in the middle, and it travels well. The in-sample optimum was 0.7 on four corpora, 0.6 on NFCorpus, 0.8 on ArguAna and 0.5 on TREC-COVID. It was never 1.0: some blending always beat dense alone on the mean. When we chose the weight on six corpora and applied it to the seventh, it picked 0.6 or 0.7 every time, and that cost at most 0.014 nDCG@10 against the in-sample best everywhere except TREC-COVID (0.031).

The best average isn't the safest weight, though, and TREC-COVID shows the trade nicely. Going from 0.7 to 0.5 raises its mean nDCG@10 from 0.717 to 0.748. It also raises the share of queries below dense-only from 14% to 26%. Which one you want depends on whether your users feel the average or the worst case.

My starting point: if dense leads, use a dense weight of 0.6–0.7, settle the exact value with cross-validation on your labeled queries, and measure the tax on queries you didn't tune on.

Won't a better encoder fix this?

It's the obvious objection: the tax is just a symptom of weak embeddings. So we scaled up the dense side. BGE-small, BGE-base and BGE-large cover 33M to 335M parameters in one family, and we added MiniLM (22M, from 2021) and Qwen3-Embedding (596M, from 2025). Step through them in Figure 5.

Better encoders raise the scores. They don't lower the tax. Across the BGE family's tenfold increase in parameters, the interpolation tax on SciFact, FiQA, NFCorpus and ArguAna moves by at most four points while nDCG@10 climbs. At BGE-large, interpolation still hurts 17–40% of queries on six corpora. Qwen3-Embedding, the newest and largest model we tried, pays 14–43%, which overlaps every older and smaller model.

Where Qwen3's tax does differ, it follows the balance between the two retrievers rather than how good the encoder is. It's highest on NFCorpus, where Qwen3 barely leads BM25, and lowest on TREC-COVID and Quora, where it leads by a lot. Fusion still beat dense-only on average on 33 of the 35 encoder–corpus pairs.

Upgrading the sparse side doesn't fix it either. Swapping BM25 for SPLADE, a stronger learned-sparse retriever, left interpolation's tax at 14–41% on six corpora and 60% on TREC-COVID, so that tax comes with score blending itself. RRF's tax does respond to how good the lexical side is, which makes sense for a method that can't tell a weak list's confident-looking ranks from a strong one's. Even with SPLADE, though, RRF still hurt 19–46% of queries.

When you upgrade the embedding model, re-measure the balance. A new encoder changes which fusion method and weight you should use. It doesn't change whether there's a tax.

Can you route around it?

If fusion fails on predictable queries, the obvious move is to fuse only when it helps. A perfect per-query router would gain 0.02–0.05 nDCG@10 over static fusion on every corpus, so the prize is real. We tried to collect it with 17 cheap features per query (query length, term rarity, score margins, how much the two lists overlap, and classic query-performance predictors like NQC and WIG) feeding a gradient-boosted classifier.

Within a corpus, failure is weakly predictable: a mean held-out AUC of 0.63, up to 0.80 on Quora. Across corpora it falls apart. Trained on six corpora and tested on the seventh, AUC averaged 0.59 and ranged from chance (0.49 on NFCorpus) to 0.74. A router that switched queries to dense-only never beat static interpolation on a held-out corpus. On three corpora it didn't route a single query, and on the two where it routed a lot of them it lost a little.

Why? The signals change meaning from one corpus to the next. The dense retriever's top-1 margin is the clearest example. Where dense dominates, a confident top hit means fusion can only dilute it, so that query is safe. On TREC-COVID, where BM25 is as strong as dense, the same confident hit predicts a failure, because the dense-heavy weight throws away BM25's evidence. Eleven of the 17 features point toward failure on some corpus and away from it on another. A router trained on one balance learns the wrong lesson for the next.

I'd pick one fusion policy per corpus and leave it there. If you do want per-query routing, test it on a corpus it wasn't trained on before you trust it.

Does a reranker pay the tax?

Most production stacks put a cross-encoder after retrieval, so maybe first-stage order doesn't matter much? To find out, we reranked the top 30 candidates from each method with BGE-reranker-v2-m3.

It helps, but only where the reranker is good at the corpus. On the three corpora where it improved every method (SciFact, FiQA and TREC-COVID), the share of queries where fusion finished below dense-only fell by a third on SciFact and not at all on the other two. Even there it mostly reshuffled who pays. On FiQA it repaired 75 of the 95 hurt queries and newly hurt 81 others. On ArguAna and SciDocs the reranker made every method worse, and the tax only “disappeared” because every list collapsed onto the reranker's own ordering.

After a good reranker, the second retriever adds very little. Once reranked, interpolated candidates scored within 0.011 nDCG@10 of dense-only candidates on six corpora, with the balanced TREC-COVID as the exception. And the costs are lopsided. On a single consumer machine (an Apple M5), a BM25 query took 1.1–4.3 ms, while the cross-encoder took about 1.6 s per query for 30 candidates. BM25 is the cheap lever and the reranker is the expensive one. Keep in mind this is one reranker at one depth, so read it as a measurement, not a ceiling.

If you already run a reranker you trust, spend your time validating it on each corpus instead of tuning fusion. If you can't afford one, fusion with a weight set from your corpus's balance is the cheap win.

What I'd do on your corpus

Here's the paper's guidance as a calculator. Plug in what you measured on your own labeled sample, or start from one of the seven corpora.

  1. Measure the balance first. Pull 200–400 real queries, spread across your traffic, and judge at least the pooled top ten from both retrievers. Then compare BM25 and dense nDCG@10 query by query. In our data the per-query differences had standard deviations of 0.17–0.36, which puts the 95% interval on the gap at about ±0.02–0.05 with 200 queries.
  2. Pick the method from the balance. If dense leads clearly (by 0.04 or more in our data, against about 0.01 on the balanced corpora), use dense-weighted interpolation at 0.6–0.7, set by cross-validation. If they're balanced, use RRF, CombMNZ or interpolation near 0.5.
  3. Keep dense-only on the table. Fusion's edge over dense-only was 0.008–0.051 nDCG@10, while 8–21% of queries lost 0.04–0.21. If whatever reads your results is rank-sensitive and you care about the worst case, dense-only might be the better product.
  4. Don't expect a new encoder to settle it. Re-measure the balance whenever you change the encoder or the corpus shifts.
  5. Budget the second retriever against the reranker. BM25 costs milliseconds per query. A cross-encoder costs a second or more.

Closing thoughts

Hybrid retrieval isn't wrong. It won on average on every corpus we tested, and I'd still reach for it first in most systems. But an average is a sum of wins and losses, and the losses land on specific queries and the people who typed them. Count both sides, pick the fusion method from your corpus's balance, and give dense-only a fair hearing.

What this doesn't cover. We measured first-stage retrieval and one reranking stage, not generated answers, so how much of the tax reaches a RAG answer depends on how rank-sensitive your reader is. The reranking results are for one cross-encoder at depth 30. TREC-COVID has only 50 queries, so its numbers are noisy. And we left out the multi-million-document BEIR corpora to keep everything runnable on a CPU.
